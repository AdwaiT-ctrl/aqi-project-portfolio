Final Conclusions

Introduction

The project starts with the public health importance of AQI and the need to make air quality patterns easy to understand. 10 research questions guide the analysis.

PCA

Principal Component Analysis reveals that 7 components capture 95% of variance in the 8-dimensional pollutant space, enabling effective dimensionality reduction.

Clustering

KMeans (k=2,3,5), Hierarchical, and DBSCAN offer complementary views of air quality patterns. KMeans with k=2 (silhouette=0.54) provides the cleanest clean-vs-poor separation.

Association Rules

Apriori mining discovers 114 co-occurrence patterns in pollutant levels, revealing that poor air quality often involves multiple elevated pollutants simultaneously.

Logistic Regression

Best accuracy: 90.93% — Clean linear decision boundary with strong probabilistic interpretation and balanced precision/recall (0.89-0.93).

Decision Trees

Accuracy: 89.68% — Highly interpretable hierarchical splits on key pollutants. Max depth=4 prevents overfitting while retaining explanatory power.

Naive Bayes

Best variant (Categorical NB): 88.71% — Different feature formats (Gaussian, Multinomial, Bernoulli) highlight the importance of preprocessing and feature interpretation.

Support Vector Machines

Accuracy: 90.93% — RBF kernel matches Logistic Regression performance through non-linear decision boundaries. Exceptional precision for poor air alerts (0.93).

Key Findings:

1. Model Performance Summit: Logistic Regression and SVM lead with 90.93% accuracy, demonstrating that pollutant-to-AQI relationships are largely smooth and well-structured. These models are production-ready for air quality forecasting.

2. Interpretability vs. Accuracy Trade-off: Decision Trees (89.68%) offer interpretable decision rules at a modest accuracy cost, making them valuable for public communication. The top pollutants driving decisions are easily explainable to stakeholders.

3. Unsupervised Learning Insights: PCA shows that pollutant concentrations cluster in lower-dimensional subspaces. KMeans and Hierarchical clustering align with the binary AQI classification, validating that natural groupings exist in the feature space before labels are applied.

4. Association Patterns: Mining frequent itemsets reveals that poor air quality rarely occurs in isolation—typically 2-3 pollutants spike together. This co-occurrence structure is critical for understanding root causes (e.g., traffic congestion elevating both PM2.5 and NO₂).

5. Feature Importance: Across all methods, PM2.5 and PM10 consistently emerge as primary AQI drivers, followed by NO₂. This aligns with domain knowledge in environmental science.

Recommendations for Deployment:

  • Primary Model: Deploy Logistic Regression or SVM for real-time air quality prediction—both achieve 90.93% accuracy with strong F1 scores.
  • Transparency Layer: Pair the predictor with Decision Tree visualizations to explain individual predictions to policy makers and the public.
  • Monitoring Dashboard: Use Clustering results to segment cities/regions into air quality "profiles" for targeted interventions.
  • Root Cause Analysis: Leverage Association Rules to identify pollution events caused by specific multi-pollutant co-occurrences.
  • Feature Engineering: Extend models with temporal features (season, hour, day-of-week) and external data (traffic volume, weather) for further improvement.

Conclusion:

This project demonstrates a complete data science pipeline: from exploratory data analysis and unsupervised learning through supervised classification and interpretation. The AQI dataset (16,010 records) provides rich signal—achieving >90% accuracy on binary air quality classification is strong evidence that machine learning can drive real-world public health decision-making. The combination of multiple algorithms, proper cross-validation, and deployment-ready preprocessing makes this portfolio a solid foundation for a production air quality monitoring system.
