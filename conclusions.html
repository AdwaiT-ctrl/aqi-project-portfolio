Final Conclusions

The biggest message from this project is simple: air quality is not random. When we look at many days together, clear patterns appear. Some cities stay mostly stable, while others rise and fall quickly. Seeing these patterns early can help people plan safer routines.

Another key takeaway is that no single daily number tells the whole story. Air quality changes with time, place, and local activity. Looking at trends over months and years gives a much better picture than checking one day in isolation.

The model sections of this site all point in the same direction: we can make useful predictions about whether a day is likely to have safer or riskier air. That does not replace health advice, but it can support practical decisions like outdoor school activity, exercise timing, and local warning alerts.

The new Module 4 work adds two important strengths. SVM helps separate clean and poor air days very clearly, and Ensemble Learning combines multiple model opinions to improve reliability. In plain terms, this means the final prediction is less likely to be thrown off by one weak signal.

The end of this story is hopeful: with regular data collection and clear communication, cities can respond earlier and people can protect themselves better. The most important outcome is not just better charts or model scores, but better decisions for everyday health.
